# 8-bit and 16-bit consoles and handhelds: primer

## Summary

This note asks how the consoles and handhelds of the 8-bit and 16-bit era work, and what that asks of an emulator.
Their processors, video chips, sound chips, and cartridges run in parallel from divided clocks,
and games change hardware state part-way through a frame, a line, or an instruction,
so the order and timing of bus accesses are visible to software.
For the NES, SNES, and Game Boy the hubs name published games that fail unless components are interleaved per line,
and often per dot or per cycle.
Three points matter most to Rustalgia: a machine is a set of chip revisions and a region, not one model;
colour and sound output are analogue models, and for some systems the sources say no single answer is correct;
and firmware dependence ranges from none to firmware every game calls,
so the questions `R0-02` records reach several systems of this era.
Among the hubs and patents this note read, no maker document was found for most of the era's custom chips,
and every processor test-vector set read was generated by an emulator.

## Scope

- Question: how do the machines of the 8-bit and 16-bit era work, and what does each subsystem ask of an emulator?
- Covered: processors; memory, buses, and cartridges; video; audio; timing, synchronisation, and input; media,
  protection, and firmware.
  The worked examples are the systems of three family surveys that depend on this item: Game Boy
  and Game Boy Color (`R1-10`), NES and SNES (`R1-15`), and Master System, Game Gear, and Genesis
  (Mega Drive) (`R1-22`).
  `R1-24` (arcade) and `R1-25` (other handhelds and home computers) also depend on this item.
  Other systems of the era appear where they differ in kind.
- Left to other items: one row for every system sold (`R1-05`); emulators of each family
  (`R1-10` to `R1-25`);
  arcade boards (`R1-24`); the law on firmware, lock-outs, and logos
  (`R0-02`, cited by section); scheduling, rendering, and audio as engineering topics (`R2-01` to `R2-15`).
- Researched on 2026-11-20; most wiki pages are pinned by revision and most repositories by commit in the Sources,
  which say where a page showed none.
- How claims were checked: research agents fetched every source on 2026-11-20 as raw text and copied quotations from
  that text.
  A second, independent pass of nine verifiers, who saw only this note,

## Findings

### What accuracy means here

- The NESdev wiki separates accuracy, matching the console on an unknown program, from compatibility with known games,
  and says accuracy cannot be measured directly; test programs and games that rely on obscure behaviour are a good,
  though not perfect, predictor (NESdev wiki, "Accuracy").
- Components run in parallel and many games rewrite video registers and cartridge banks several times a frame:
  "This switching must be fairly fine-grained" (NESdev wiki, "Catch-up").
- The Game Boy, which drives its own LCD, can pause pixel output;
  home consoles "are on a schedule imposed by the screen they are hooked up to" (Pan Docs, "Rendering", footnote).
- The NES and Master System derive their clocks from multiples of the 3.579545 MHz colour subcarrier
  (NESdev wiki, "CPU"; SMS Power, "Clock Rate"),
  but the Neo Geo AES's 24.167829 MHz is not a whole multiple
  (NeoGeo Development Wiki, "Framerate"; calculated),
  and the Game Boy's 4.194304 MHz and the WonderSwan's 12.288 MHz are unrelated to television
  (Pan Docs, "Specifications"; WSdev wiki, "Timing"; inference from the rates).
- None of the frame rates found is exactly 60 Hz or 50 Hz: NES NTSC 60.0988 Hz
  (NESdev wiki, "Cycle reference chart"),
  SNES NTSC 60.09880627 Hz (fullsnes, "SNES Timing Oscillators"; not re-checked), Game Boy 59.73 Hz
  (Pan Docs, "Specifications"),
  Neo Geo MVS 59.1856 Hz, which its wiki says needs scope measurements
  (NeoGeo Development Wiki, "Framerate"), WonderSwan by default about 75.47 Hz (WSdev wiki, "Timing").
  A host display cannot show these one-to-one, so frame pacing or resampling absorbs the difference (inference).

### Processors

#### The 6502 family

- The NES CPU is a Ricoh 6502 core without decimal mode, in the RP2A03 (NTSC) or RP2A07 (PAL)
  (NESdev wiki, "CPU", "2A03").
  Die-photograph research found the decimal correction disabled by removing a few transistors
  (Breaks, "Ricoh", in an unofficial machine translation of the Russian page);
  the D flag can still be set, but arithmetic stays binary (NESdev wiki, "Status flags").
  MOS Technology patented the decimal adder (US 3,991,307).
- Every 6502 cycle is a bus read or a bus write
  (NESdev wiki, "CPU"),
  so a cycle-accurate core performs the dummy reads and writes,
  which matter where they hit registers with side effects (inference).
- The NMOS 6502's decode logic is defined for 151 of 256 opcodes; the rest do strange things,
  among them mixtures of two instructions, nothing at all, or crashing the processor
  (Wikipedia, "MOS Technology 6502"), and some halt the CPU until reset (NESdev wiki, "CPU unofficial opcodes").
  NESdev says an accurate NES emulator must implement every instruction, official or not,
  and lists games that use the unofficial ones (NESdev wiki, "CPU unofficial opcodes").
  For one opcode the result "will depend on the exact model of CPU, the variations of chip manufacture,
  the power supply and the temperature"
  (Visual6502 wiki mirror on NESdev, "6502 Opcode 8B (XAA, ANE)"),
  so an emulator picks a model of it and documents it (inference).
- WDC's datasheet lists how its CMOS W65C02S differs from the NMOS part: undefined opcodes are fixed-length NOPs,
  read-modify-write does two reads and one write instead of one read and two writes, D is cleared on interrupt,
  and an interrupt during a BRK fetch lets the BRK run first (WDC W65C02S datasheet, Table 7-1).
  A CMOS core is a different model, not an option on the NMOS one (inference).
- "65C02" names several instruction sets: the SingleStepTests suite ships separate sets for the MOS 6502, the NES 6502,
  and the Synertek, Rockwell, and WDC 65C02s
  (SingleStepTests 65x02 READMEs), so each system must name its variant (inference).
- The PC Engine (TurboGrafx-16)'s HuC6280 is a 65C02-based Hudson CPU with an on-chip timer, sound generator,
  and MMU of eight mapping registers giving a 21-bit address space in 8 KiB pages, and runs at 1.79 MHz or 7.16 MHz
  (Wikipedia, "Hudson Soft HuC6280", a page tagged as having one source, a Hudson manual hosted by a third party).
  MacDonald, who gives the low speed as 1.78 MHz, says the CSL and CSH instructions switch the speed,
  and reports from tests that block-transfer instructions cannot be interrupted, so an interrupt waits until one ends
  (MacDonald, "TurboGrafx-16 Hardware Notes", sections 1 and 2; lead).
- NES games depend on cycle detail: *Battletoads* on the page-crossing penalty,
  *Cobra Triangle* on a dummy read acknowledging an APU interrupt,
  and *Spelunker* on the interrupt poll point when NMI is enabled while the vertical-blank flag is already set
  (NESdev wiki, "Tricky-to-emulate games").

#### The 65C816 and the SNES

- The SNES S-CPU is the Ricoh 5A22: a WDC 65C816 core plus multiply and divide units, DMA and HDMA, joypad ports,
  and interrupt timing, with a 24-bit "Bus A" and an 8-bit "Bus B"
  (SNESdev wiki, "65C816"; Wikipedia, "Ricoh 5A22", a page tagged as having one source).
- WDC's datasheet gives the 65C816's extra cycles for 16-bit operation, a non-zero direct-page low byte, page crossing,
  and taken branches, and separate vectors for native and emulation mode
  (WDC W65C816S datasheet, Table 3-1 and Table 5-7 notes, Tables 5-2 and 5-3).
  The SNES resets into emulation mode (SNESdev wiki, "CPU vectors"), so both modes need full timing (inference).
- On the SNES a CPU cycle takes 6, 8, or 12 master clocks by address and fast-ROM setting, internal cycles taking 6
  (SNESdev wiki, "Timing"):
  the datasheet counts cycles, and the console sets each cycle's length by the address accessed (inference).
- Revisions differ: the first 5A22 can crash when a DMA ends right before HDMA,
  and the second can fail a DMA after a recent HDMA to the display register (SNESdev wiki, "Errata").
  The errata page cites forum threads, the open-tier higan and bsnes code, and the closed SNES Development Manual;
  these two items carry no citation, so they need another source (inference).
  Items it cites to higan or bsnes were written after reading an implementation.

#### The Z80 and the SM83

- The Z80 runs the Master System and Game Gear
  (SMS Power, "Clock Rate", "Interrupt Mechanism"),
  and the SG-1000 and ColecoVision (from memory), and is the sound CPU of the Genesis
  (MacDonald, "Sega Genesis hardware notes", section 1.3; lead) and Neo Geo (NeoGeo Development Wiki, "Clock").
  The Master System divides a 53.6931 MHz crystal (NTSC) by 15 for the CPU and sound chip (SMS Power, "Clock Rate").
- The Z80's maskable interrupt has three modes, one reading a vector from the data bus
  (SMS Power, "Interrupt Mechanism"), so on an undriven bus the vector is a property of the console (inference).
  The Master System wires NMI to its Pause button (SMS Power, "Pause Button").
- After SCF and CCF, flag bits 5 and 3 depend on whether the previous instruction changed the flags,
  a fact found in 2012; the behaviour differs between Zilog and some other makers' parts and on some boards is unstable
  (z80test readme).
  A Z80 core keeps hidden state, including an internal register called MEMPTR
  (z80test readme),
  which the SingleStepTests vectors record as WZ beside two flag-tracking bits
  (SingleStepTests z80 README; that WZ is MEMPTR is from memory).
  z80test compares results with "values obtained from a real 48K Spectrum with Zilog Z80 CPU"
  (z80test readme), the only hardware-derived Z80 oracle among the test sets read (z80test, SingleStepTests z80).
- The Game Boy's core "hasn't publicly been given a name by either Sharp or Nintendo";
  Gekkio identifies it from Sharp datasheets and data books, which are closed pending `R0-05` option 5, as Sharp's SM83,
  "or at least something that is 100% compatible with it" (Gekkio, "GB: Complete Technical Reference", chapter 4).
- The SM83 lacks the Z80's I/O instructions, index and alternate registers, and the DD, FD, and ED prefix groups,
  keeping most of the CB group, and adds its own loads; "The unused (-) opcodes will lock up the Game Boy CPU when used"
  (Pan Docs, "CPU Comparison with Z80").
  It makes at most one memory access per four-clock machine cycle
  and overlaps each fetch with the previous instruction's last cycle
  (GB: Complete Technical Reference, sections 2.2 and 6.1).
- Game Boy interrupts: EI takes effect one instruction late, dispatch takes five machine cycles,
  and HALT with interrupts disabled and one pending usually reads the next byte twice,
  with exceptions after EI and before RST (Pan Docs, "Interrupts", "halt").

#### The 68000

- Motorola's manual sets a bus cycle of "four clock periods
  (eight bus cycle states)"
  plus wait states, with instruction times as total clocks and read and write counts
  (Motorola, "M68000 User's Manual", ninth edition, sections 5.8 and 8, on NXP's site;
  its title page conveys no patent licence).
  Multiply and divide times depend on the operand (same, section 8.3), so timing depends on data.
- Interrupts have seven levels checked between instructions, level 7 unmaskable,
  and an odd word access raises an address error (same, sections 6.3.2 and 6.3.10).
  Plutiedev says many emulators skip address errors, so bugs go unnoticed until the program runs on a console
  (Plutiedev, "Hardware issues"; lead).
- The Genesis runs the 68000 at about 7.67 MHz and the Z80 at about 3.58 MHz on NTSC, by MacDonald's notes,
  which say everything in them was checked on hardware (MacDonald, "Sega Genesis hardware notes", section 1.3; lead).
  Sega Retro gives a 53.693175 MHz master clock, citing a Sega service manual hosted as a closed file
  (Sega Retro, "Sega Mega Drive/Technical specifications");
  dividing it by 7 and 15 gives both rates (calculated from the rates).
- Plutiedev reports that on most consoles TAS does not write back, while one later Genesis board does
  (Plutiedev, "Hardware issues"; lead).
- The Neo Geo runs its 68000 at 12 MHz, and cartridge pins select wait states that some games depend on
  (NeoGeo Development Wiki, "Clock", "Wait cycle").
- The Sega CD (Mega CD)'s second 68000 runs at 12.5 MHz by Wikipedia ("Sega CD") and 12 MHz by the MegaDrive Wiki
  ("Mega CD"); the sources read do not settle it.

#### Others, and what they share

- The Intellivision's CP1610 has 10-bit instruction words
  (Wikipedia, "General Instrument CP1600"),
  the WonderSwan's NEC V30MZ follows the 80186 and stalls for DMA
  because only the CPU's quarter of the memory-access cycle can reach the cartridge bus
  (WSdev wiki, "NEC V30MZ", "Timing"),
  and for the Neo Geo Pocket's TLCS-900/H no maker manual was found among Wikipedia's references,
  whose Toshiba product page was not opened, and the register closes or walls the two test sets
  (Wikipedia, "Neo Geo Pocket"; register).
- Interrupt sampling differs per processor: the 6502 delays an IRQ one instruction after CLI, SEI, and PLP but not RTI
  (NESdev wiki, "CPU interrupts"),
  the SM83's EI is late (Pan Docs, "Interrupts"), the 68000 checks between instructions
  (Motorola manual, section 6.3.2), and the V30MZ delays after prefixes (WSdev wiki, "NEC V30MZ interrupts").
  Each core needs its own sampling point (inference).
- Clocks change at run time: HuC6280 speed (MacDonald; lead), Game Boy Color double speed
  (Pan Docs, "CGB Registers"), and SNES cycle length by address; Neo Geo wait states differ by cartridge.
  A CPU cycle is not a stable unit of time; a master clock is (inference).

### Memory, buses, and cartridges

#### Open bus and mirrors

- On the NES, "reading open bus repeats the last value that was read from the bus before this read",
  and controller ports drive only some bits, so the rest repeat the previous read, usually the address high byte,
  which some games need (NESdev wiki, "Open bus behavior").
- On the SNES, open bus decays in about two frames by a forum test, and the 5A22 has an internal data bus of its own
  (SNESdev wiki, "Open bus").
  On the Game Boy's external bus, pull-up resistors make undriven reads return 1 bits,
  unless bits were driven low shortly before (GB: Complete Technical Reference, "Undriven bus values").
  On the Genesis, reads of one unused range return the high byte of the next instruction fetch,
  and another range locks the machine (MacDonald, "Sega Genesis hardware notes", section 1; lead).
  Open bus is a property of each console, not of the CPU family (inference).
- Incomplete decoding repeats memory, such as NES work RAM every 2 KiB
  (NESdev wiki, "Mirroring"), and test programs probe the mirrors (AccuracyCoin README).

#### NES cartridges

- The cartridge owns `0x4020`–`0xFFFF`, sees nearly every CPU access, and routes the PPU's pattern and nametable fetches
  (NESdev wiki, "CPU memory map", "PPU memory map").
- A "mapper" maps memory into both spaces and may add save RAM, scanline interrupts, sound, or arithmetic;
  the community numbers them in a catalogue that NES 2.0 extends (NESdev wiki, "Mapper", "NES 2.0").
  A Nintendo patent filed in 1987 describes a cartridge controller taking bank numbers from the CPU (US 4,949,298).
- Mappers reach into CPU timing: the MMC1 ignores the data bit of a write on the cycle after another,
  though never its reset bit, and two games depend on the two halves of that rule
  (NESdev wiki, "MMC1"),
  and the MMC3 counts scanlines from a PPU address line, with revisions differing (NESdev wiki, "MMC3").
- Writes to some discrete mappers meet ROM driving the bus; the wiki recommends the AND of both values,
  from a forum test (NESdev wiki, "Bus conflict").
- The console reads its reset and interrupt vectors from the cartridge and no header
  (NESdev wiki, "CPU memory map"; that no header exists is inference);
  NES 2.0 exists to remove "the need to use ROM checksums, or other information outside the header"
  (NESdev wiki, "NES 2.0"), so emulators rely on file headers and catalogues.
  Whether a board has save RAM must be carried too: a few games misbehave if RAM appears where they expect open bus
  (NESdev wiki, "Open bus behavior").

#### SNES memory and coprocessors

- The SNES has a 24-bit address space; RAM and I/O mirrors sit in banks `0x00`–`0x3F` and `0x80`–`0xBF`,
  and banks `0x7E`–`0x7F` hold the whole 128 KiB of work RAM (SNESdev wiki, "Memory map").
  Sound RAM belongs to the sound CPU, which the main CPU reaches only through four ports
  (SNESdev wiki, "S-SMP"; fullsnes, "SNES Memory", not re-checked).
- Cartridges use LoROM, HiROM, or ExHiROM layouts; the internal header "is not needed by the SNES hardware",
  so emulators score candidate offsets to find it (SNESdev wiki, "Memory map", "ROM header").
- General DMA stops the CPU until it completes (SNESdev wiki, "DMA registers"), at 8 master clocks per byte
  (SNESdev wiki, "Timing"),
  and HDMA writes registers at horizontal blank on the lines its table selects (SNESdev wiki, "DMA registers").
  A Nintendo and Ricoh patent describes DMA by
  which "it is possible to change display data during the horizontal blanking period without the intervention of the
  CPU" (US 5,291,189); that this is HDMA is inference.
- Cartridges add processors: the SA-1, Super FX, Cx4, DSP-n chips, and decompressors, each with its own map
  (fullsnes, "SNES Cartridges"; not re-checked).
  The DSP-n chips have internal program ROM
  (fullsnes, "SNES Cartridges"; not re-checked),
  so low-level emulation needs chip firmware, a closed dependency
  (inference; `R0-02`, "Firmware, keys, and game copies").
  The SNESdev Super FX page cites the closed SNES Development Manual
  (SNESdev wiki, "Super FX"), so facts from it need another source.

#### Game Boy memory

- The map holds two ROM windows, video, external, and work RAM, an echo range, sprite memory, I/O, and high RAM;
  the Color banks video and work RAM (Pan Docs, "Memory Map").
  Of the echo range, "Nintendo prohibits developers from using this memory range", with no source given (same page).
- One unused range reads differently on each hardware family
  (same page),
  and video memory is blocked to the CPU while the video chip uses it
  (Pan Docs, "Accessing VRAM and OAM"), so the model changes memory behaviour (inference).
- The header names the cartridge's controller; controllers add a real-time clock, rumble, flash, EEPROM,
  or an accelerometer (Pan Docs, "MBCs" and controller pages),
  so saves span three memory types and a clock must keep time between sessions (inference).
- Sprite DMA uses its own address decoding, so the CPU's accesses during it resolve by bus
  (GB: Complete Technical Reference, "DMA"; Pan Docs, "OAM DMA Transfer").

#### Sega memory

- Sega's 8-bit mapper, "used in 99% of software", switches 16 KiB slots by writes that also land in RAM
  (SMS Power, "Mappers");
  other mappers exist and the header does not name them
  (SMS Power, "ROM header"), so detection needs a catalogue (inference).
  A port switches BIOS, card, cartridge, and expansion slots into one range (SMS Power, "Port $3E").
- The Genesis's 68000 must take the Z80's bus before touching Z80 RAM,
  and the Z80 sees 68000 space through a 32 KiB window
  (MacDonald, "Sega Genesis hardware notes", sections 2.2 and 2.3, lead; MegaDrive Wiki, "Zilog Z80").
  Either CPU can stall the other (inference).
- During video DMA from 68000 memory "the 68000 is frozen"
  (MacDonald, "Sega Genesis VDP documentation"; lead),
  and a source cannot cross a 128 KiB boundary (Plutiedev, "DMA transfer"; lead); each fact rests on one lead.
  For a length of zero, MacDonald gives `0xFFFF` and Plutiedev 65536
  (Plutiedev, "Hardware issues"; lead); no test found settles it.
- Saves use battery RAM on odd bytes (Plutiedev, "Saving progress with SRAM"; lead) or a serial EEPROM in a few games
  (MacDonald, "Sega Genesis hardware notes", section 4.1; lead).
- The Sega CD's word RAM is handed between its two 68000s, changing both maps (MegaDrive Wiki, "Mega CD").
- Elsewhere, the WonderSwan's "SoC features an unified memory architecture"
  (WSdev wiki, "Memory map"), and the Lynx copies cartridge data to RAM before use (Wikipedia, "Atari Lynx").

### Video

#### NES

- NTSC has exactly 3 PPU dots per CPU cycle, PAL 3.2, and "Dendy" clones 3 again
  (NESdev wiki, "Cycle reference chart"), so a game meets three timing profiles by region (inference).
  One dot is skipped on odd frames while rendering is on, so frame length depends on a register
  (NESdev wiki, "PPU frame timing").
- While rendering is on, the PPU fetches at fixed dots of each line
  (NESdev wiki, "PPU rendering"),
  and cartridge chips watch those fetches
  (NESdev wiki, "MMC2", "MMC3");
  *Marble Madness* switches character banks mid-line (NESdev wiki, "Tricky-to-emulate games").
- After eight sprites, a bug in the overflow search gives false results
  (NESdev wiki, "PPU sprite evaluation"),
  and sprites are prioritised among themselves before the background, which *Super Mario Bros.
  3* uses (NESdev wiki, "PPU sprite priority").
  Games poll the sprite-0 hit flag to time scroll splits; *Battletoads* hangs without it on the right line
  (NESdev wiki, "PPU registers", "Tricky-to-emulate games").
- "Unlike many other game consoles, the NES does not generate RGB or YUV and then encode that to composite"
  (NESdev wiki, "NTSC video");
  its artefacts change with the line and frame, so a palette gives flat colour
  and a signal model reproduces them (inference).
  The wiki concludes that "no single composite palette can match the intended display of every possible game title"
  (NESdev wiki, "PPU palettes").
- NTSC pixels have an 8:7 aspect ratio on a 4:3 display (NESdev wiki, "Overscan").

#### SNES

- A line is normally 1364 master clocks, with short or long lines on some fields and a 40-clock refresh pause
  (SNESdev wiki, "Timing").
- Eight background modes, changeable mid-screen; 128 sprites with per-line limits of 32 sprites and 34 slivers;
  the sprite limit drops the highest OAM indices, and the sliver limit, evaluated in reverse, the lowest
  (SNESdev wiki, "Backgrounds", "Sprites"), which the wiki takes from a forum test and the open-tier bsnes code.
- Colour maths blends a main and a sub screen, and windows hide layers or the blend per line
  (SNESdev wiki, "Color math", "Windows"), so the renderer keeps two screens per line (inference).
- 512-pixel output blurs on composite televisions, and some games rely on that to blend alternating columns
  ("pseudo hi-res") (SNESdev wiki, "Backgrounds").
- Mode 7 maps the screen to a background through a matrix of four 8.8 fixed-point values
  (SNESdev wiki, "Mode 7 transform"),
  in an arrangement a Nintendo and Ricoh patent describes
  (US 5,327,158; its link to the SNES is inference);
  pixel-exact output depends on rounding the pages read do not give (inference).
- The one-chip SNES has a sharper picture and first-line glitches in some games
  (SNESdev wiki, "Version differences"), so a correct picture is relative to a revision (inference).

#### Game Boy

- A frame is 154 lines of 456 dots; the drawing period lengthens with scroll, window, and sprites,
  shortening horizontal blank (Pan Docs, "Rendering"), so interrupt timing depends on what is drawn (inference).
  The chip draws through FIFOs (Pan Docs, "Pixel FIFO"),
  the level of model that reproduces mid-line effects (inference).
- At most 10 sprites per line; overlap priority is by X in monochrome mode and by table order in Color mode
  (Pan Docs, "OAM").
  On the original a status-register write can raise a spurious interrupt that two games need (Pan Docs, "STAT").
- The Mealybug Tearoom tests check mid-line changes; their expected images come from the author's emulator,
  with photographs of devices alongside (Mealybug Tearoom README).
- The Color's colours look different on an sRGB screen without correction (Pan Docs, "Palettes").

#### Sega

- Four Sega video chips descend from the TMS9918 and treat its legacy modes differently;
  MacDonald names Master System games that fail on the Genesis's chip
  (MacDonald, "Sega Master System VDP documentation", sections 1 and 13; lead).
  The first chip zooms only the first four of eight sprites on a line horizontally; the rest are doubled vertically
  (same, section 10; lead).
- The Game Gear squeezes each Master System line into its LCD using colour sub-elements,
  and drops rows by a method MacDonald could not determine (same, section 14; lead).
- The Genesis caches some sprite attributes, which *Castlevania: Bloodlines* exploits (Plutiedev, "Sprites"; lead).
  Colour-memory writes during display show as dots
  (MegaDrive Wiki, "VDP"), which some Sonic games hide under ripple sprites (Plutiedev, "Sonic water"; lead).
- Kabuto's notes, from "running my own tests and guesswork",
  give a fixed per-line pattern of memory slots and the DMA speeds that follow
  (Kabuto, "SEGA Mega Drive / Genesis hardware notes", on Plutiedev; lead).
  The colour DAC is not linear; Plutiedev gives approximate levels, explains the curve from the die,
  and says they differ by revision (Plutiedev, "VDP color ramp"; lead).
- MacDonald's Genesis video document says "All the register names are taken from the manual"
  (MacDonald, "Sega Genesis VDP documentation", section 17);
  this note takes that to be Sega's closed manual (inference), so facts known only from it need a second source.

#### Others

- Hudson's patent describes the PC Engine's sixteen-per-line sprite limit:
  "the seventeenth sprite which is designated to be displayed is not displayed"
  (US 4,951,038); that it is the PC Engine is inference.
  MacDonald's notes give three software-chosen dot clocks
  (MacDonald, "TurboGrafx-16 Hardware Notes", section 6; lead)
  but swap two chip numbers relative to Wikipedia ("TurboGrafx-16").
- The Neo Geo composes each line into line buffers from sprites that "can't be zoomed, they can only be shrunk"
  (NeoGeo Development Wiki, "Line buffers", "Sprites").
- The Atari 2600 holds one line of graphics and stops the CPU until the line ends on request
  (Wikipedia, "Television Interface Adaptor"), so CPU timing is display timing (inference).
  The WonderSwan has "no canon palette" (WSdev wiki, "Display/Palette"), and the Vectrex draws vectors
  (Wikipedia, "Vectrex").
- Across the worked examples the observable unit is at least the dot; a line renderer runs most titles, and the NES,
  Game Boy, and Genesis sources name titles that need more (inference).

### Audio

- The era's sound hardware is of four kinds: tone generators, Yamaha FM, sample playback by a sound processor,
  and samples the CPU writes to a DAC or volume register; the last couples sound to CPU timing (inference).

#### NES

- The APU sits in the CPU chip with two pulse channels, a triangle, noise, and a sample channel (NESdev wiki, "APU").
  The triangle can run above hearing, which the filters average away
  (NESdev wiki, "APU Triangle"), so naive sampling aliases (inference).
- The sample channel fetches by DMA; on NTSC a fetch during a read of a register with read side effects,
  such as a controller port, causes extra reads that can lose data, and the PAL 2A07 avoids it (NESdev wiki, "APU DMC").
  Its frame sequencer is not synchronised with video (NESdev wiki, "APU Frame Counter").
- Mixing is non-linear: "Each channel has its own internal digital-to-analog convertor (DAC),
  implemented in a way that causes non-linearity and interaction between channels" (NESdev wiki, "APU Mixer").
  The wiki's approximate formula comes from blargg's APU reference,
  and its filters from the circuit and community analysis (same page).
- Famicom cartridges add sound chips (NESdev wiki, "Category:Expansion audio"); one outputs channels in turn,
  audible as a whine when all eight are enabled, and one runs on its own oscillator
  (NESdev wiki, "Namco 163 audio", "VRC7 audio").

#### Game Boy

- Two pulse channels, a 4-bit wavetable, and noise, sequenced by a bit of the divider register,
  so writing it changes sound timing (Pan Docs, "Audio", "Audio Details").
  "The length of APU ticks is not affected by CGB double speed" (Pan Docs, "Audio").
- Each channel has a DAC, the high-pass filter differs by model, and switching causes pops
  (Pan Docs, "Audio Details");
  wave memory access differs by model
  (Pan Docs, "Audio Registers"),
  and one length quirk by revision, breaking at least one game (Pan Docs, "Audio Details", "Obscure Behavior").

#### SNES

- The sound module is a second computer: an SPC700 core at 1.024 MHz with a 64-byte boot ROM,
  a DSP with eight sample voices, and 64 KiB of RAM (SNESdev wiki, "S-SMP").
  It runs from its own 24.576 MHz resonator;
  the rate "varies from console to console anywhere from 32000 Hz to 32160 Hz",
  and one game misbehaves at the nominal clock
  (SNESdev wiki, "S-SMP", "APU pinout", citing community measurements), so the sound clock is an option (inference).
- The CPU and DSP share RAM by time-slicing (SNESdev wiki, "S-SMP"), an arrangement a Sony patent describes
  (US 5,761,643; its link to the SNES is inference).
  Samples are 9-byte blocks with prediction filters
  (SNESdev wiki, "BRR samples"), a layout another Sony patent describes (US 5,086,475; the link is inference).
- Voices pass a 4-point Gaussian interpolation
  (SNESdev wiki, "S-DSP registers"),
  whose fixed table appears to come from chip ROM and is not recorded here (inference).
  Echo writes into sound RAM and can overwrite other data there, including zero page (same page).
- "SPC timings are inaccurate (requires SPC to run 1 cycle at a time, rather than 1 instruction at a time)" heads a list
  of failing games (SNESdev wiki, "Tricky-to-emulate games").

#### Sega and others

- The SN76489 is discrete in the SG-1000 and ColecoVision and built into Sega's later video chips;
  its noise shift register is 15 or 16 bits by chip version (SMS Power, "SN76489").
  Its outputs leak towards zero, and "SMS1 and Mega Drive consoles have the proper 2dB step gapping,
  whereas the SMS2 clips due to a hardware design mistake" (same page, resting on a forum thread).
  Games play samples by timed volume writes (SMS Power, "Sample Playback").
- The YM2413 FM chip is built into the Japanese Master System and was sold in an add-on for the Mark III
  (SMS Power, "YM2413"; Sega Retro, "FM Sound Unit"); its fixed instruments are chip ROM content, not recorded here.
- The YM2612 has six 4-operator FM channels, the sixth able to play samples through a DAC-enable register
  (Wikipedia, "Yamaha YM2612"; Sega Retro, "YM2612", which the second pass could not reach),
  and outputs about 53267 samples a second on NTSC
  (calculated from Plutiedev's cycle counts in "ym2612-operations" and the 68000 clock above; no maker figure found).
  The original chip's DAC distorts quiet sounds and later variants reduced it, so some games sound different by model
  (Wikipedia, "Yamaha YM2612"; Sega Retro, "Sega Mega Drive/Technical specifications").
- A Hudson patent describes six 32-step wavetable channels with stereo attenuators, noise, modulation,
  and a direct output mode (US 4,924,744; that it is the PC Engine's chip is inference),
  the one maker description of a tone generator of the era among the patents read.
- The Neo Geo's YM2610 mixes tone channels outside the chip with unknown relative gain
  (NeoGeo Development Wiki, "YM2610"; inference).
  The WonderSwan outputs at a fixed 24000 Hz (WSdev wiki, "Sound"), and the 2600's sound runs from a 31399 Hz base
  (Wikipedia, "Television Interface Adaptor"), about twice its line rate (inference).

#### What audio asks of an emulator

- Native rates are not host rates, so every system needs a resampler, and multi-chip systems merge streams first
  (inference from the rates above).
  Dynamic rate control adjusts the ratio slightly so that "ideally, the audio buffer is never underrun nor overrun"
  (libretro documentation, "Dynamic Rate Control").
- Where sound feeds back into CPU timing, as with NES sample DMA, the Game Boy divider, and the SNES sound CPU,
  audio cannot be rendered afterwards from a log of writes (inference).

### Timing, synchronisation, and input

#### Scheduling

- The NESdev wiki describes interleaving one CPU cycle at a time; "catch-up",
  running the least predictable component ahead to the next possible interaction and bringing the others up to it;
  and scanline emulation, which it calls a crude form of catch-up that runs well-behaved games
  (NESdev wiki, "Catch-up").
  Catch-up can be refined by predicting events and timestamping writes,
  and with several CPUs that interrupt each other the page describes running ahead from a checkpoint and rewinding
  as one way (same page).

#### NES

- The master clock is "six times that of the color subcarrier,
  but this frequency is about 24% higher on PAL than on NTSC"
  (NESdev wiki, "Cycle reference chart"), so region is a machine parameter (inference).
- The CPU and PPU start each power-on in one of several relative alignments, and the page's own results,
  for the NTSC PPU only, assume one (NESdev wiki, "PPU frame timing"); "At power-on,
  whether the first CPU cycle is get or put is random" (NESdev wiki, "DMA").
  A deterministic emulator chooses both (inference).
- Reading the status register on the dot vertical blank begins suppresses that frame's NMI (NESdev wiki, "NMI").
  Sprite DMA halts the CPU for 513 or 514 cycles.
  A halted 2A03 repeats its last read, and with sample DMA that gives joypads extra reads,
  which console models count differently (NESdev wiki, "DMA").
- AccuracyCoin tests these, and "was designed for an NTSC console with an RP2A03G CPU and RP2C02G PPU"
  (AccuracyCoin README): a hardware oracle for one revision.
- The controller is a shift register read one bit per access
  (NESdev wiki, "Standard controller");
  the Four Score and the Hori adapter identify themselves by a signature in four-player mode
  (NESdev wiki, "Four player adapters").
  The wiki models the Zapper's light reading as decaying "over the course of several scanlines"
  (NESdev wiki, "Zapper"), so a light gun needs beam position in emulated time and a display without lag (inference).

#### SNES

- fullsnes lists the events of a frame by beam position
  (fullsnes, "SNES Timing H/V Events"; not re-checked); its origin for that list is not stated.
- "The S-SMP runs on its own clock, independent of the main CPU" (SNESdev wiki, "Timing").
  The two CPUs talk through four ports, and a read on the cycle of a write returns a wrong value
  (SNESdev wiki, "S-SMP"), so the handshake spans two clock domains (inference).
  Several cartridge chips add their own crystals (fullsnes, "External Oscillators (in Cartridges)"; not re-checked).
- Automatic joypad reading runs for about the first three lines of vertical blank, starting a short time after it begins
  (SNESdev wiki, "Controller reading"),
  and the Super Scope latches the beam counters, which games calibrate for (SNESdev wiki, "Super Scope").
  The SNESdev multitap page rests mainly on the closed SNES Development Manual and is not used here
  (SNESdev wiki, "Multitap").

#### Game Boy

- A frame is 70224 dots at 2^22 Hz (Pan Docs, "Specifications", "Rendering"; calculated from the rates).
  The NTSC Super Game Boy runs about 2.4% fast and the PAL one about 1.5% fast from the SNES clock,
  while the Super Game Boy 2 has its own crystal (Pan Docs, "SGB Functions").
- The status interrupt fires on a rising edge of the OR of its sources, so one source can block another
  (Pan Docs, "Interrupt Sources"),
  and the timer counts falling edges of an internal counter bit, so writing the divider can add a tick
  (Pan Docs, "Timer Obscure Behaviour"); both are circuits to model per machine cycle (inference).
- Mooneye's author ran its tests on every listed SoC revision: "in the end I only trust real hardware"
  (Mooneye Test Suite README).
- A link port set to the external clock waits indefinitely for the other console's clock
  (Pan Docs, "Serial Data Transfer (Link Cable)"),
  so linked emulators synchronise two schedulers at bit-clock granularity (inference).

#### Sega

- The Genesis's video chip in its Master System mode holds the interrupt until its control port is read,
  and its line counter's reload decides which line interrupts;
  MacDonald expects the same of the Master System but measured the Genesis (MacDonald, `vdpint.txt`; lead).
  The Genesis's chip stalls the 68000 on a fifth buffered write during display
  (MacDonald, "Sega Genesis VDP documentation", section 6, quoting another researcher; lead).
- The Master System's light gun latches the horizontal counter
  (SMS Power, "Light Phaser"),
  the Japanese paddle runs on its own clock (SMS Power, "Paddle"), and region shows through port wiring
  (SMS Power, "Region Detection").
- The six-button pad relies on timing for its compatibility with the three-button protocol:
  "Do not read the controllers more than once per frame!" (Plutiedev, "Controllers"; lead).
- A video DMA started while the Z80 uses the 68000's bus can load garbage, so developers stop the Z80 during DMA
  (Plutiedev, "Hardware issues"; lead).

#### Others

- The PC Engine's timer runs at a fixed rate while its CPU changes speed
  (MacDonald, "TurboGrafx-16 Hardware Notes", section 2.2; lead).
  The Neo Geo has a pixel-clock timer for raster effects (NeoGeo Development Wiki, "Timer interrupt").
- Some SNES cartridges and the Neo Geo MVS keep real time from their own crystals
  (fullsnes, "External Oscillators (in Cartridges)", not re-checked; NeoGeo Development Wiki, "Clock");
  whether such clocks follow host or emulated time matters to save states and fast-forward (inference).

### Media, protection, and firmware

#### Headers

- The Game Boy header is the console's own: the boot ROM of most models checks a logo and a checksum,
  the Super Game Boy's leaving the check to its SNES program,
  and the header names the controller and Color and Super Game Boy support
  (Pan Docs, "The Cartridge Header", "Power Up Sequence").
  The logo's bytes are not recorded here.
- The SNES header serves developers and tools
  (SNESdev wiki, "ROM header"), and copier files add 512 bytes that loaders strip (SNESdev wiki, "ROM file formats").
- The Master System header begins with the 8-byte string "TMR SEGA",
  which the export Master System and Game Gear BIOSes require
  (SMS Power, "ROM header", "BIOSes"),
  and of the consoles only the export Master System BIOS checks its region field
  (SMS Power, "ROM header"), so emulators should not reject games on its values (inference).
- On the Genesis, "If it doesn't find the initial 'SEGA' text in this field,
  systems with TMSS will refuse to boot the game" (Plutiedev, "ROM header reference"; lead).

#### Boot ROMs and firmware

- Firmware dependence falls into three groups
  (inference from the findings below; the Atari 2600, HuCard, Intellivision, and Lynx placements are from memory):
  none, where the cartridge supplies the reset vector
  (NES, SNES main CPU, Atari 2600, SG-1000, PC Engine HuCard);
  a boot ROM that checks and hands over
  (Game Boy family, export Master System, Genesis with TMSS, WonderSwan),
  which for the Game Boy and WonderSwan an emulator can skip from documented post-boot state;
  and firmware that games call or that loads them
  (Famicom Disk System, the SNES sound boot ROM, Super Game Boy, Satellaview, PC Engine CD, Sega CD, Neo Geo,
  Intellivision, Lynx).
- Nine official Game Boy boot ROMs are known by model, and games detect models by the registers they leave
  (Pan Docs, "Power Up Sequence", "SGB Unlocking"), so a boot-ROM-less emulator still chooses a model.
  Pan Docs says its post-boot tables "were obtained from analysis of the boot ROM's disassemblies,
  and confirmed using Mooneye-GB tests"
  (Pan Docs, "Power Up Sequence");
  they rest on firmware analysis and on tests run on consoles, and the page warns that "some of it may contain errors"
  (same page).
- The Color's boot ROM colours monochrome games from a table chosen by title
  (same page);
  Pan Docs points only to the boot ROM's disassembly, TCRF (walled), and a replacement boot ROM (closed),
  so no description of the table was found that does not come from the boot ROM.
- The SNES sound CPU starts in a 64-byte boot ROM that accepts a program by handshake
  (SNESdev wiki, "S-SMP", "Booting the SPC700");
  every game depends on it (inference), and SNESdev does not say where its protocol description comes from;
  the code and the closed SNES Development Manual are both possible origins (inference).
- "The first version of the Game Gear, the Mark III and all SG-1000 and SC-3000 variants have no BIOS"
  (SMS Power, "BIOS");
  SMS Power's account of later BIOS checks rests on disassembly, by the excerpts on its page
  (SMS Power, "BIOSes"; inference).
- The Neo Geo system ROM holds "various generic functions which can or must be used by games"
  (NeoGeo Development Wiki, "System ROM"),
  and WSdev documents the WonderSwan's state after its boot ROM exits
  (WSdev wiki, "Boot ROM"), from which an emulator could start (inference).
- The Famicom Disk System's BIOS provides routines games call
  (NESdev wiki, "FDS BIOS"),
  so without it an emulator reimplements them, a firmware reimplementation
  (inference; ADR 0001 closes others' versions).
  The common disk file omits the gaps and checksums of real disks
  (NESdev wiki, "FDS file format", "FDS disk format"), which an emulator then synthesises (inference).
- NEC released System Card upgrades for the PC Engine CD along with new disc formats
  (Wikipedia, "TurboGrafx-16"); that a game needs a given card version is inference.
  The Sega CD has a BIOS but a cartridge can drive its CPU directly
  (Wikipedia, "Sega CD"; Plutiedev, "Using the Sub-CPU in mode 1"; lead),
  and Satellaview broadcasts ended in 2000 with most content lost (Wikipedia, "Satellaview").

#### Lock-out

- The NES lock-out is a microcontroller in console and cartridge running one program;
  on a wrong response the console's reset line is pulsed at 1 Hz (NESdev wiki, "CIC lockout chip").
  Nintendo's patent: "The key device and the lock device are synchronized with each other,
  executing the same arithmetic operation according to the same program" (US 4,799,635).
  The SNES uses the same scheme (fullsnes, "SNES Cartridge CIC Lockout Chip"; not re-checked);
  an emulator emulates neither chip (inference).
- The Game Boy compares character data held in the console with data in the cartridge
  (US 5,134,391; Pan Docs, "Power Up Sequence");
  an emulator running no boot ROM makes no comparison and holds no logo copy, as `R0-02` infers
  ("Trade marks and branding, recorded as risks").
- Later Game Gears and the Famicom Disk System check for a string or message and show a licence notice
  (SMS Power, "BIOSes"; NESdev wiki, "FDS BIOS"),
  and the Genesis's TMSS does the same by the Ninth Circuit's account in Sega v Accolade, which `R0-01` lists.
  Atari required 7800 cartridges to be digitally signed
  (Wikipedia, "Atari 7800"); that the BIOS verifies the signature is from memory, and no key is described here.
- `R0-02` covers the law and is not repeated: no § 1201 decision found on a cartridge-era lock-out
  ("Anti-circumvention law in the United States"),
  the boot-logo questions ("Firmware, keys, and game copies"), and options 4, 5, 6, and 16.
- Open hubs hold firmware listings beside their prose,
  on the NeoGeo wiki's "Security code" and "Boot process" pages and SMS Power's "BIOSes" page
  (research log); none of it was used.

### Tests and their oracles

- No single-step processor test set captured from hardware was found for the 6502 family, Z80, SM83, 68000, HuC6280,
  SPC700, or TLCS-900H; the READMEs and descriptions name MAME, ares, Mesen, JSMoo, or an unnamed implementation
  (SingleStepTests READMEs and repository descriptions; the TLCS-900H set is closed and was read as prose only).
  "Any bugs that exist in Mame's microcoded M68000 emulator will exist here too"
  (SingleStepTests m68000 README), and the HuC6280 set is "only as good as Mesen" (SingleStepTests huc6280 README).
  Passing such a set shows agreement with that emulator (inference).
- The hardware-derived oracles found are test programs (z80test, AccuracyCoin, Mooneye), plus MacDonald's notes (lead).
  No open, hardware-referenced audio test was found beyond AccuracyCoin's NES checks
  (AccuracyCoin README; gilyon snes-tests `spctest` README; Mooneye README).

## Options and trade-offs

### Timing granularity

- **What:** interleave and render per frame, per line, per dot, or per cycle.
- **Costs:** finer units cost host time (inference); coarser ones break named games on the NES, SNES, and Game Boy,
  while no Sega title found needs finer than per-line.
- **Gains:** per-line runs well-behaved titles
  (NESdev wiki, "Catch-up");
  per-dot and per-cycle are what the timing entries in the tricky-games lists need (inference).
- **Used by:** the NESdev wiki describes per-cycle and per-scanline emulation as practised,
  and per-frame as hiding raster effects; the family surveys record which emulators use which.

### Lockstep or catch-up

- **What:** step every component together, or run one ahead and catch the others up at points of interaction.
- **Costs:** lockstep costs speed; catch-up relies on predicting interactions and may timestamp writes,
  and with several CPUs that interrupt each other the wiki describes rewinding to a checkpoint as one way.
- **Gains:** lockstep is simple to reason about; catch-up is faster where predictions hold.
- **Used by:** the NESdev wiki names one emulator for lockstep and describes the others without naming users.

### Master-clock or per-component time

- **What:** count all time in system master clocks, or keep a cycle count per component.
- **Costs:** master clocks still need separate domains for the SNES sound unit and cartridge crystals;
  per-component counts convert at every interaction (inference).
- **Gains:** master clocks follow run-time speed changes without conversion (inference).
- **Used by:** no emulator's design was read for this note.

### Revisions and models

- **What:** one chip revision per system, or a selectable set.
- **Costs:** each revision adds behaviour to test; tests such as AccuracyCoin target one.
- **Gains:** revisions are visible to software on the NES, SNES, and Game Boy;
  for Sega only leads report it (inference).
- **Used by:** Mooneye's test names say which models each test should pass on; the SNESdev errata name revisions.

### Open bus and hardware faults

- **What:** model open bus, bus conflicts, and known faults, or return fixed values and omit faults.
- **Costs:** modelling needs per-console data, some from leads; omitting breaks games that rely on the behaviour.
- **Gains:** fidelity on one side, freedom from undocumented faults on the other.
- **Used by:** Plutiedev reports that many emulators skip address errors and hardware delays,
  and that early ones mishandled zero-length DMA (lead).

### Test oracles

- **What:** single-step vectors generated by other emulators, or program-level tests run on hardware.
- **Costs:** generated vectors encode an emulator's errors and measure similarity to it; hardware tests cover less
  (inference; Mooneye keeps an "emulator-only" class it finds hard to verify on hardware).
- **Gains:** vectors check state and, in some sets, each bus cycle, within the generator's limits;
  hardware tests check the console.
- **Used by:** the SingleStepTests authors and the test-suite authors named above.

### Firmware

- **What:** for each system, start from documented post-boot state, require a user's image, or write a replacement.
- **Costs:** Game Boy post-boot state rests partly on firmware analysis; user images raise `R0-02`'s questions;
  replacements must come from hardware descriptions and tests, since others' are closed under ADR 0001.
- **Gains:** skipping needs no image for the second group; a replacement removes it for the third.
- **Used by:** `R0-02` options 4 and 5 list projects that require an image or ship a replacement;
  no project list was found for starting from post-boot state.

### Colour and sound output

- **What:** fixed palettes and linear mixing, or signal, LCD, DAC, and filter models, possibly selectable.
- **Costs:** models cost host time (inference) and rest on community measurements;
  no single palette is correct for the NES's composite output or the WonderSwan.
- **Gains:** fidelity to what players saw and heard; selection avoids choosing one answer.
- **Used by:** the NESdev, Pan Docs, and Plutiedev (lead) pages describe models;
  the surveys record which emulators offer them.

## Implications for Rustalgia

- The family surveys (`R1-10`, `R1-15`, `R1-22`) should record each emulator's timing granularity, modelled revisions,
  and test oracles, where the findings show emulators differ; this feeds `R1-90`.
- A master-clock scheduler with separate clock domains is one design these findings allow;
  the choice belongs to `R2-01` and `R2-03`.
- Rendering granularity and selectable colour models feed `R2-04` and the quality targets (`R6-02`);
  resampling and rate control feed `R2-06` and `R2-07`.
- The three firmware groups set which systems need an image, a replacement, or nothing;
  this feeds `R2-12` and an ADR on firmware, with `R0-02` as the legal input.
- Emulator-generated test vectors measure similarity to their generators, which bears on `R0-06` and `R2-15`.
- Hubs mix prose with firmware listings and facts from closed manuals,
  so later notes cite at page level and say when a fact rests on such material.

## Open questions

- What does a Genesis video DMA of length zero do?
  A hardware test; none was found.
- Does the Sega CD's second 68000 run at 12 MHz or 12.5 MHz?
  `R1-22`.
- Which SNES sound clock should be used, given console-to-console variation?
  `R2-03` and the owner.
- Can Game Boy post-boot state be established from tests alone?
  `R1-10`, or a spike that runs Mooneye's boot tests.
- How is Game Boy Color colourisation known other than from its boot ROM?
  None found; the owner decides whether firmware-derived tables may be used.
- Is there an open source for the PC Engine beyond MacDonald's notes and Hudson's patents, and for the Atari consoles,
  Intellivision, ColecoVision, and Neo Geo Pocket beyond Wikipedia?
  `R1-05` for the home consoles, which no family survey covers
  (observed in the roadmap),
  and `R1-25` for the handheld; candidate sites are unregistered or refused access (research log).
- Are there hardware-captured processor vectors for any processor of this era?
  None found; a spike could capture some on owned hardware.
- What do Sega Retro's technical pages say beyond the three read, and do those three read the same today?
  The site served a bot check to later automated requests on 2026-11-20, including the second pass (research log).
